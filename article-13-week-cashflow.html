Cash-flow planning

How to build a useful 13-week cash-flow forecast

A short-horizon forecast is a working control: it helps a business see when cash may be tight, which assumptions need checking and who must act next.

PRAVI BUSINESS ADVISORS LLPPractical guide

A cash-flow forecast is not the same as a profit forecast. Profit may include revenue that has not been collected and expenses that have not yet been paid. A 13-week view focuses on expected cash movements over the next quarter and should be updated as actual receipts and payments become known.

1. Reconcile the opening cash position

Start with bank balances and cash balances as at a defined cut-off. Reconcile them to bank statements and identify restricted balances, uncleared payments and material transfers. Do not use an unexplained accounting balance as if it were available cash.

2. Forecast receipts using evidence and timing

List customer receipts by expected date, amount and confidence level. Use invoice due dates, customer commitments, collection history and known disputes. Separate contracted or highly probable receipts from optimistic assumptions, and update the date when new evidence arrives.

3. Separate committed payments from flexible spending

Include payroll, taxes, rent, loan instalments, supplier commitments and other expected outflows. Distinguish contractual or operationally essential payments from discretionary spending. Do not assume a statutory due date or a lender arrangement without verifying the facts and current terms.

4. Make the weekly view actionable

For each week, show opening cash, expected receipts, expected payments and closing cash. The next week's opening cash should agree with the prior week's closing cash. Flag the minimum projected cash point and the week it occurs.

5. Create a downside case

Model a plausible delay in major collections, lower sales receipts or an unexpected payment. State the assumptions clearly. A downside case is a planning scenario, not a prediction; it helps management decide what evidence to gather and which contingency actions to prepare.

6. Assign actions and update weekly

  • Give each material receipt or payment an owner.
  • Record the source and last-updated date for major assumptions.
  • Compare forecast with actual cash movements and explain material differences.
  • Document decisions, owner and target date for each action.
  • Roll the forecast forward each week instead of treating it as a one-time spreadsheet.
Illustrative example: if a major customer receipt moves from week 3 to week 6, update the expected date and review the intervening minimum cash balance. Do not leave the receipt in week 3 simply because that was the original invoice expectation.

Minimum review checklist

  • Opening cash reconciles to current bank information.
  • Receipts have evidence, expected dates and confidence levels.
  • Payments include known commitments and current obligations.
  • Weekly opening and closing balances roll forward correctly.
  • Base and downside assumptions are visible.
  • Forecast-to-actual variances and actions are documented.
